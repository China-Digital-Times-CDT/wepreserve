⚖️ Joshua Wong|黃之鋒

Related: Faces of the Crackdown | The Hong Kong 47 | Hong Kong Alliance | Chow Hang-tung | ⚖️ Jimmy Lai

Joshua Wong Chi-fung in 2017. Photo: Voice of America, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Joshua Wong Chi-fung (黃之鋒, born 13 October 1996) is the best-known figure of Hong Kong's youth protest movement — the schoolboy activist who became the global face of the 2014 Umbrella Movement and, a decade later, one of the most heavily prosecuted people under the National Security Law. He has been in custody since 2020, is serving a sentence in the Hong Kong 47 subversion case, and in September 2026 pleaded guilty to a second national-security charge that carries a maximum of life imprisonment.

The student who took on Beijing

Wong first came to prominence at 15. In 2011 he co-founded the student group Scholarism (學民思潮); in 2012 he led the campaign against a compulsory "moral and national education" curriculum critics saw as pro-Beijing indoctrination, drawing a rally of some 100,000 people and forcing the government to shelve the plan.

Two years later he was a leading face of the 2014 Umbrella Movement, the 79-day occupation demanding genuine universal suffrage. His arrest during the storming of Civic Square helped trigger the sit-ins. The image of a bespectacled teenager facing down Beijing made him internationally famous — named to Time's "25 Most Influential Teens," later the subject of the Netflix documentary Joshua: Teenager vs. Superpower (2017), and in 2018 nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize alongside fellow Umbrella leaders.

Demosistō and disqualification

In 2016 Wong co-founded the pro-democracy party Demosistō (香港眾志) with Nathan Law and Agnes Chow, campaigning for Hong Kong's right to self-determination. He was repeatedly barred from running for or holding office. On 30 June 2020, hours before the National Security Law took effect, Wong, Law and Chow quit the party and Demosistō disbanded — its parting message: "We will meet again."

A decade in and out of prison

Even before the NSL, Wong was jailed again and again over the protests he led — for the 2014 storming of Civic Square, for contempt over the Mong Kok occupation, for organising the June 2019 siege of police headquarters during the anti-extradition-bill movement, and for attending banned June 4th vigils. He has been continuously imprisoned since December 2020.

The Hong Kong 47

Wong was among the Hong Kong 47 — the democrats prosecuted for organising the 2020 opposition primary election. He pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit subversion and, in November 2024, was sentenced to 4 years 8 months. He had been due for release in 2027.

A second case — up to life

On 6 June 2025, while already in prison, Wong was hit with a fresh charge: conspiracy to collude with foreign forces. Prosecutors say that in 2020 he lobbied foreign governments — chiefly the United States — to impose sanctions or a blockade on Hong Kong and China, and kept up that campaign after the NSL made it a crime.

On 2 September 2026 Wong pleaded guilty to the charge at the High Court. The offence carries three to ten years, rising to up to life imprisonment if the court finds it "of a grave nature" — meaning the 29-year-old, rather than walking free in 2027, could face many more years behind bars (HKFP · Al Jazeera · RFA). Sentencing is expected later.

Even the film is under review (2026)

Wong's story reached a global audience through Joshua: Teenager vs. Superpower (2017), the 78-minute Netflix documentary by American director Joe Piscatella that follows his Scholarism years and features Nathan Law and Agnes Chow. Nearly a decade on, the film itself became a target. On 15 September 2026, Hong Kong's Security Bureau said it was "taking follow-up action" over the documentary — still streamable in the city, rated 13+ — stressing that "all films broadcast in the city must meet Hong Kong law," including the National Security Law and the 2024 Safeguarding National Security Ordinance. Whether any act amounts to a crime, it said, "depends on the facts and circumstances of each case" and "cannot be judged in the abstract"; if violations were found, "law-enforcement agencies will act according to the law" (Dim Sum Daily).

Back in 2020, as the NSL took effect, Netflix had signalled it would not "proactively" pull the film and would act only on a formal government takedown request (HKFP). The review extends the city's censorship drive — already felt by its bookshops and the Tiananmen vigil — to an international streaming platform, and raised the prospect that merely hosting a film about a jailed activist could be cast as a security offence. The film sits within the archive's wider record of Hong Kong protest films.

Recognition

Wong's activism made him one of the movement's most decorated figures abroad — Time's most-influential-teens list (2014), Fortune's "World's Greatest Leaders" (2015), a 2018 Nobel Peace Prize nomination, and the co-author of Unfree Speech: The Threat to Global Democracy and Why We Must Act, Now (2020), with a foreword by last colonial governor Chris Patten and an introduction by artist Ai Weiwei. To Beijing he became a symbol of "foreign-backed" subversion; to his supporters, of a generation jailed for asking to choose its own government.
