One Year On

Parent: 🔥 Hong Kong Fire 2025

The first anniversary of the Wang Fuk Court fire falls on 26 November 2026 — one year after the blaze killed 168 people in Tai Po, Hong Kong's deadliest fire since 1948. This page follows how that anniversary is marked: the commemorations and the pressure brought to bear on them, and the survivors and families a year on. The immediate aftermath of 2025 — the three-day citywide mourning, the 頭七, and the first diaspora vigils — is recorded under Vigils and Mourning; the fuller story of the cause, the report, accountability, resettlement and the criminal case lives across the collection hub and its pages.

Commemoration, vigils and their policing

  • 2026-11-12 — Memorial Masses under police scrutiny: As the anniversary approached, Hong Kong police reportedly questioned two Catholic churches over planned Wang Fuk Court memorial Masses; one parish replaced its anniversary Mass with a general ceremony for the year's dead. Cardinal Stephen Chow (周守仁) responded by inviting every parish in the diocese to offer a Mass for the fire's victims on 26 Nov 2026. Chief Secretary Eric Chan (陳國基) said lawful memorials would not be blocked, while warning that people "with ulterior motives" were promoting memorial gatherings online to draw crowds into "disruptive acts." The Tai Po parish (Immaculate Heart of Mary) briefly pulled its Mass publicity — Auxiliary Bishop Joseph Ha (夏志誠) called the removal a "misunderstanding" and confirmed police had queried the parish — while a Wan Chai priest, Fr. Law Kwok-fai (羅國輝), said a self-described police "friend" came to "show concern" about his Mass, which he found "too disturbing" (HKFP · SCMP · The Pillar · BBC Chinese).
  • 2026-11-21/22 — Migrant-worker survivors' PolyU event cancelled: A book launch and stage performance by Indonesian migrant domestic workers who survived the fire — the second volume of What We Carry, Under the Same Sky, with poetry, drama and a prayer of mourning — set for 22 Nov at Hong Kong Polytechnic University (Room ST111), was cancelled less than a day beforehand. Organisers Microlab Collective (a cross-disciplinary platform tied to PolyU's English & Communication department) and the Indonesian Migrant Workers Union (印尼移工聯盟, JBMI) cited only "unforeseen circumstances," gave no reason, and said they hoped to reschedule. The year-long workshop had gathered survivors' essays, poems, photographs and artwork into the book; participants had rehearsed on their days off, and some bereaved workers' families had planned to attend. The silenced work was the survivors' own testimony — a 30-minute play they wrote, directed and performed themselves — of months of insomnia and "seeing red" on closing their eyes, of guilt over a cat left behind in the flames, of a childhood friend (Erawati) who died in a stairwell after a last FaceTime to say goodbye to her son, closing on the line they had written: "you are all mothers, who came to a faraway place to work for your children." The fire killed 10 foreign domestic workers among its 168 dead and affected 225 Indonesian and Filipino helpers (集誌社 The Collective · HK01 · 法庭線 The Witness).
  • 2026-11-24 — One year on, mourning turns cautious (BBC): With the anniversary two days away, Chief Secretary Eric Chan (陳國基) confirmed the government would hold no official commemoration but would not block lawful mourning, while Security chief Chris Tang (鄧炳強) said only that "any lawful activity will be allowed." Hong Kong Economic Journal reported that the government and the pro-establishment camp leaned against any ceremony — a mourning crowd might become a gauge of public anger over the fire — and that political and business figures and opinion leaders had been "advised" not to attend Wang Fuk commemorations. Bereaved relatives told the BBC they would return to the site alone and without announcing a time, fearing any gathering would be branded "趁機搞事" (exploiting the occasion to make trouble); some residents said they had been followed or photographed by unidentified people and had asked reporters to delete earlier criticism of the government. A flower-tribute zone was marked out at 廣福休憩處 (Kwong Fuk Rest Garden), but officials would not confirm who drew the lines or what the anniversary would hold (BBC Chinese).
  • 2026-11-24 — Anniversary coverage pulled; the "以災亂港" framing (BBC · ReNews): Newsroom staff told the BBC that a finished anniversary special was pulled before its scheduled broadcast, and that executives at another station had instructed reporters to avoid interviewing "people" (人). ReNews then published a recording (27 Nov) of what it said was Now News chief Chan Tit-biu (陳鐵彪) explaining at an internal meeting why he had spiked the station’s current-affairs strand 《經緯線》 (The Pulse) — a feature interviewing three Wang Fuk residents, due to air 22 Nov. In it he calls the fire "a national-security incident" and the piece "extremely negative," worries it would "encourage people to protest … and disrupt the peace," and brands a resident who refuses the buyout as "soft resistance" (軟對抗) who is "not wiped out" (唔係死絕種). After the recording drew wide criticism, Chan apologised that evening — admitting he had "misspoken" — to his staff and to the victims and their families. The chill tracks Beijing's framing: the Office for Safeguarding National Security and the Hong Kong and Macao Affairs Office have cast those pressing questions over the fire as seeking to "以災亂港" — to use the disaster to throw Hong Kong into chaos — the same language used when a student petition-organiser was arrested in late November 2025 (recorded under Activism & suppression) (BBC Chinese · ReNews, 27 Nov 2026)).
  • 2026-11-25 — The Cathedral reads the 168 names: On the eve of the anniversary, the Cathedral of the Immaculate Conception (聖母無原罪主教座堂) in Central held a memorial Mass for the fire’s 168 dead and for the injured and all affected residents, the victims’ names set before the altar. Presiding, Fr. Dominic Chan (陳志明) said the names were read out because the dead "are not an overall figure, but unique lives"; four bereaved relatives then read all 168 names aloud, one by one, over about eight minutes. It was among the Masses Cardinal Stephen Chow had asked every parish to offer for the victims on the anniversary (The Pillar).
  • 2026-11-26 — One year on: mourners return to Wang Fuk Court: On the anniversary, residents and the public came through the day to lay flowers at 廣福休憩處 (Kwong Fuk Rest Garden) and along the fences and cordon lines around the estate — white chrysanthemums, paper cranes strung on the wire by Tai Po students, cards (one reading "杰仔，爸爸來看你了" — Kit-chai, Daddy came to see you), and, from performance artist Sanmu (三木 / 陳式森), the character "沉" brushed in water on the ground: "silence, grief, sinking, unredressed injustice." Bereaved relatives returned — Lee Chun-ho (李俊豪), surviving uncle of the family that lost four of its seven members, broke down and cried toward the towers, "你哋好走喇" (go well now); Yip Ka-kui (葉家駒) laid white roses marked "毋忘愛" (don’t forget love) for his late wife; and a survivor surnamed Tse, in a hard hat, mourned the 167 residents and the fallen firefighter Ho Wai-ho (何偉豪). The refrain was the same — "還168個死者嘅真相" (the truth owed to the 168 dead) — a year on, with no official yet bearing criminal responsibility (獨立媒體 InMedia).
  • 2026-11-26 — Mourners’ flowers cleared as fast as they were laid: Through the afternoon and into the night, Civil Aid Service members repeatedly boxed up the flowers almost as soon as they were placed and cordoned the tribute ground with orange tape, saying it was to "free up space" and that the flowers would all be taken to a Taoist temple (蓬瀛仙館, Fung Ying Seen Koon) — a plan a pastor questioned on behalf of Christian and Buddhist mourners. The crowd protested — "又話合法都得嘅" (you said lawful was fine), "有返啲良心喇" (have some conscience) — some wept; Polly, whose mother died in the fire, said it felt as if "the gift to my mother was being taken away." Mourners then reclaimed the flowers and relaid them themselves in the garden’s pavilion and corners, some recalling that police had carted off citizens’ donated relief supplies "like rubbish" the year before. The government defended the clearing in a statement that night, saying officers had boxed the bouquets only to "vacate more space" as more mourners arrived and to "properly preserve the condolences from various sectors," with the tributes to be "progressively relocated to religious venues." Police kept a deliberately low-key presence, after Security chief Chris Tang said lawful memorials were allowed but warned that some might try to "hijack" the day in acts that could amount to "sedition" (獨立媒體 InMedia · HKFP).

Survivors and families, one year on

  • A bereaved family (集誌社, 16 Nov 2026): In a video record titled "IT'S OK TO BE NOT OK", the survivors of a family that lost four of its seven members — the grandmother, the father, 15-month-old "Yan Yan" (欣欣) and their foreign domestic worker (the Wang Tai House family whose seven-hour rescue call went dead on the 14th floor) — described a year lived through grief: sorting the dead's belongings, psychiatric treatment, and visits to the cemetery. The surviving uncle 李俊豪, the mother "Ali" and the grandfather said the trauma of losing family has never healed, and called for a memorial monument (紀念碑) to be raised at Wang Fuk Court so that this "man-made disaster" (人禍) warns future generations (集誌社 The Collective).
  • Yip Ka-kui and other bereaved relatives, a year on (BBC · AP): Relatives who lost family described mourning quietly, out of fear. Yip Ka-kui (葉家駒, 68), whose wife Pak Sui Lin (白瑞蕮) was among the 168 killed, said he would return to the estate on the anniversary but would not tell anyone the time, wanting only "a quiet space … to mourn" and dreading any incident that could be pinned on mourners; he called the burnt-out towers a "恥辱柱" (pillar of shame) the government would rather demolish to make the matter "end." A year on he still sends his late wife WhatsApp voice messages and photos — "I just felt that maybe she’d gone out somewhere … so I kept talking to her every day" — and, returning six months ago with a bouquet to the flat they had shared for over two decades, stood in the dark and told her, "Don’t stay here. Go wherever you want to; you’re free now." Of the report and its promised reforms he asked why "it is us, the victims, who are left to pay the price." Lee Kwok-hung (李國鴻), who lost his mother and the family’s two domestic workers, said he would go back alone — "I haven’t arranged with anyone … you don’t want to let them catch you out." (Yip and Lee also appear in the fire’s report.) (BBC Chinese · AP).
  • Phyllis Lo, who lost her mother (AP): Phyllis Lo (49) spent the year mourning, sitting through the judge-led inquiry’s hearings and following the fight over long-term housing — "I lost part of my youth … I had to put my own work aside." The government’s buyback, which most owners have accepted and which rules out rebuilding on the original site, leaves her unconvinced: "I lost a root. This is my family home. I have lived here for 40 years; it was a place I could fall back on when something happened." (AP).
  • Christine, a student who lost her mother — "true death is being forgotten": At the anniversary gathering, Christine (a pseudonym), a Form 5 student whose mother died in the fire, said the crowd of mourners made her feel that people still cared and that Wang Fuk Court was not forgotten: "Someone said true death is when you are forgotten — so as long as someone still remembers this, it means they have not left. They are still in our hearts." (端傳媒 Initium)
