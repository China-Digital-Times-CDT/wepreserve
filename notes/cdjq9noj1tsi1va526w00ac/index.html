The Report & the Reckoning

Parent: 🔥 Hong Kong Fire 2025

On 22 October 2026 the judge-led Independent Committee submitted its final report on the Wang Fuk Court fire to Chief Executive John Lee. A redacted public version was released on 29 October 2026 — and the same day the government staged its reckoning: Lee led seven officials in a bow of apology, one bureau chief resigned, and a package of reforms was announced. This page collects the report itself (with preserved copies), what it withholds and why, and what the government did when it landed.

What the report is

  • Two volumes, 16 chapters. The English edition runs to 1,208 pages (Volume 1: 505 pp; Volume 2: 703 pp); the report was also published in Traditional and Simplified Chinese. Authored by The Hon. Mr Justice David Lok, Mr Chan Kin-por and Dr Rex Auyeung, dated October 2026.
  • A searchable record, not a scan. The published PDFs carry a full text layer, so the public version can be read and searched in full — valuable for preservation.
  • Released by the government, hosted by the committee. The redacted version sits on the committee's own site; the government announced the release on 29 October (committee report page; government release).

What is redacted — and why it matters

The front page of each volume carries the same notice:

"With a view to avoiding any prejudice (actual or perceived) to the relevant criminal prosecutions, certain parts of the Index, Executive Summary and Chapters 4, 6, 10 to 15 of the main body of this Report have been redacted."

The notice says certain parts of these sections are redacted — not that the chapters are gone. All of the listed chapters that we checked (4, 6 and 11–15) are present in the published volumes with their body text, including Chapter 6 on the causes of the fire and Chapter 11 on the responsibility and accountability of government bodies. The Standard counted passages redacted on more than 40 of the 1,013 pages (two volumes, 16 chapters and nine annexes), mostly in Chapters 10 and 11, with 34 redactions in the section on contractors' responsibilities (The Standard). The archive has not yet mapped these itself.

Chapters the notice lists as having redacted partsChapters the notice does not list

Chapter 4 itself explains the division of labour: it sets out facts, while "the responsibilities of various stakeholders are examined in Chapters 10 and 11" — two of the chapters the notice lists as partly redacted.

The full report already exists — it was handed to the Chief Executive unredacted on 22 October. The redactions are tied to the live prosecutions (seven people and two firms face charges including manslaughter), and the government says the full report will be released once all related criminal proceedings have ended (The Standard). Until then, this redacted version is the record — and if even it is ever taken down or further trimmed, a preserved copy becomes the archive's treasure. (See Preserved copies below.)

The apology — and the single resignation

When the redacted report was released on 29 October, at the very start of the press conference Chief Executive John Lee led seven senior officials in a formal bow of apology to the victims and the public.

On sourcing this image. These are stills from the government's own press-conference webcast (ISD) — official footage of its own officials, credited to the Information Services Department. The widely-circulated agency photographs of the same moment (AP, Reuters) are not rehosted here, in keeping with the archive's rule against reusing news-agency photos.

Only one official was held politically accountable: Secretary for Housing Winnie Ho (何永賢). She offered her resignation on 28 October; Lee accepted it, began the process of asking Beijing to formally remove her, and named Deputy Financial Secretary Michael Wong (黃偉綸) as acting housing chief. The logic: the Independent Checking Unit — found to bear the heaviest responsibility among four faulted government bodies — sits under her bureau.

Lee framed holding a single official to account as "my personal political decision," a line widely criticised as too little when the committee faulted four departments (the ICU, the Fire Services, Buildings and Labour Departments). Before the release, Executive Council member Ronny Tong had floated a "no-fault, no-accountability" framing. Separately, the Public Service Commission formed an Investigation Team to examine the individual responsibilities of the civil servants who oversaw the fire-risk aspects of the works (government release) (BBC中文; HKFP: Ho to step down).

The demands for more resignations

Winnie Ho's departure did not settle the question. The report faulted four bodies, each under a different minister, and three of those ministers stayed. Bereaved residents want all four gone, and reporters pressed the three repeatedly on 29 and 30 October.

What the bereaved and residents are asking.

  • Yip Ka-kui (葉家駒), whose wife died in the fire, called one resignation a restaurant's "minimum charge" and said the ministers over all four faulted bodies should step down: "I will not accept it." He also said Ho should have apologised in person if she truly accepted responsibility.
  • Lee Kwok-hung (李國鴻), who lost his mother and two domestic workers: "It wasn't just one department that made mistakes... Why is only one minister bearing the consequences?"
  • Betty Ho, a resident, said Ho resigned rather than being sacked, and asked how that amounted to accountability: "She has just quit her job."
  • Phyllis Lo, who lost her mother, said one official stepping down, voluntarily rather than as a penalty, was not enough, and that justice had not yet been done.

Not all residents agreed: one surnamed Tam told TVB he had not expected any senior official to resign and urged people not to focus on why only one had gone (HKFP; The Witness; 太報).

The government's answer. John Lee called it his "personal political decision", based on each official's role, their subordinates' performance and the work ahead, and said the three must follow it and carry out the reforms. Asked about his own accountability, he said that as the person with first responsibility he must lead the government through the problems and the reforms (The Witness; The Witness: Linn and Sun; The Witness: Tang).

Who defended it. Beijing's Hong Kong and Macao Affairs Office praised the government's sense of responsibility and warned against those it accused of using the disaster to stir up discontent (The Witness). Executive Council member Ronny Tong and former NPC Standing Committee member Tam Yiu-chung also backed the outcome, HK01 reported (HK01).

What happens next: civil servants. A separate Public Service Commission investigation is due within three months. It will treat about 40–50 civil servants across the four bodies as subjects and involve about 100 overall. Penalties range from a warning to dismissal, and pensions can be withheld or cut for those about to retire (The Witness). Yip also says the inquiry's picture has "missing pieces", because some witnesses never testified, and that he will study the report with his lawyers (The Witness).

The reforms announced

Alongside the report, the government set out a reform package, to be steered by a committee chaired by the Chief Secretary:

  • Criminalise bid-rigging in building maintenance — a bill targeted for the Legislative Council by year-end; the Competition Commission floated jail terms of 7–10 years.
  • Scrap and replace the Independent Checking Unit, criticised in the report for multiple failings and a "slack attitude."
  • Create a new Building Maintenance Support and Services Authority to help owners manage major renovation works.
  • Stand up a cross-agency task force (police, ICAC, Competition Commission) against collusion in maintenance projects.
  • Amend the Buildings Ordinance to end the industry's self-regulation and raise penalties.

(HKFP: new authority & bid-rigging offence; HKFP: ICU to be replaced).

The families' verdict

The bereaved were not placated. Yip Ka-kui (葉家駒) — whose wife Bai Shui Lin (白瑞蓮) died in the fire while helping neighbours escape — said he could not accept only one bureau chief stepping down: every policy secretary the report named should go, the heads of the Fire Services and Labour Departments included. He derided the government's selective accountability as refined to the "nanometre grade" — "when there's credit, the bureau chief shares in it; when something goes wrong, it's the people below who are at fault." Preventing a recurrence, he argued, demands "reform of the whole system" and an end to the departments' buck-passing (卸膊) culture, not patched-up clauses.

Mr So (蘇先生), who lost both parents, had himself tried to raise the alarm — and his story is one of the 180 pre-fire complaints the report says six departments passed around (see Investigation). In September 2024 he twice telephoned the Fire Services Department about the risk of the foam boards sealing the windows — invoking London's Grenfell Tower fire — only to be told there was no specific regulation on window-covering materials and to ask the Buildings Department instead. "A flammable thing stuck to the outside of your home — how is that not the fire service's concern?" he said; an ordinary resident cannot be expected to know the departments' division of labour. Even his father, in his eighties, had warned after a 2025 fire at Central's Wah Mau building that Wang Fuk's foam-sealed windows would be lethal. Like the others he rejected the lone resignation — Winnie Ho did not herself bow, and quitting is not the same as being held to account — and condemned the eleven-month wait for an apology ("investigation and apology do not conflict"). He wants the government to hold an annual official memorial as a formal admission of its failures.

Lee Kwok-hung (李國鴻), who lost his mother and two domestic helpers, doubted the reforms would bite: pointing to earlier anti-collusion measures that failed to stop bid-rigging — a 2013 maintenance scheme and the Urban Renewal Authority's 2016 "招標妥" tender safeguard — he questioned whether the new Building Maintenance Support and Services Authority would do any better. The two spoke after the contractors' criminal case — Prestige Construction, Will Power Architects (鴻毅) and seven directors on 25 charges including manslaughter — was adjourned at West Kowloon Magistrates' Court to 14 January 2027 (Will Power, in liquidation, still had no legal representation) (InMedia 獨立媒體).
