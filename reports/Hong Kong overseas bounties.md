# Bounties follow Hong Kong's critics into exile

Between July 2023 and October 2026 Hong Kong's national security police put **34 people living abroad** under arrest warrants with cash rewards attached. They did it in four rounds: 8 people on 3 July 2023, 5 on 14 December 2023, 6 on 24 December 2024 and 15 more on 25 July 2025. The official police feed checked on 20 October 2026 still lists exactly those 34 cases and **no new name since July 2025** ([police NSD feed](https://www.police.gov.hk/info/appeals_public/nsc/en.xml)). The people named are former legislators and district councillors, lawyers, a trade unionist, a pollster, journalists and YouTube commentators, student organisers, and the organisers and elected members of an unofficial overseas "Hong Kong Parliament". Several are citizens of the US, Canada or Australia. The Hong Kong government alleges secession, subversion and collusion with foreign forces, but almost every allegation concerns things these people said, lobbied for or testified about abroad. **None of the 34 has been arrested, and no reward has been reported paid.** Because the government cannot reach them, the pressure falls on people it can reach. From 2024 the Safeguarding National Security Ordinance let the Secretary for Security declare 29 of the 34 "specified absconders", cancelling passports and making it a crime to deal with their money. Dozens of relatives in Hong Kong have been taken in for questioning. In February 2026 Anna Kwok's father, **Kwok Yin-sang**, became the first relative convicted, jailed for eight months over an insurance policy he had bought for her when she was a toddler. Abroad, the same campaign shows up as "wanted" letters sent to neighbours, sexualised deepfakes, and a UK spy case that ended in convictions. The UK, US, Canada, Australia, the EU and the G7 have condemned it as transnational repression, but only the US has sanctioned officials. The next flashpoints are close: the Interpol General Assembly in Hong Kong in November 2026, and the expiry of the December 2023 and December 2024 rewards on 11 and 23 December 2026.

> **How to read this report.** Every offence named here is **the Hong Kong government's allegation**. None has been tested in a court with the accused present. Reward amounts appear only as facts in the record, never next to a person as a caption. Verification flags from the research notes are kept: *(snippet)* means the fact was read in a search-result excerpt because the page blocked the crawler; *(weak)* means it rests on Wikipedia or a single minor outlet; *(unverified)* means do not publish without checking. Safety rules: no home addresses, towns, workplaces or birthdates. Relatives appear by relationship only, except Kwok Yin-sang, whom mainstream outlets name because he was charged and convicted. Coverage: the police feed is current to 20 October 2026. The international-response notes run to 15 October 2026. The families notes run to June 2026. The profile notes run to about August 2026. Treat later gaps as "not covered", not as "nothing happened".

## Thirty-four names in four rounds, and not one arrest

The campaign is best read as a record of official acts. Each round paired court-issued warrants with a police reward notice, and each reward has since been renewed rather than allowed to lapse. Police said rewards are payable only on arrest and "satisfactory prosecution", which the feed defines as a "case to answer" finding ([police NSD feed](https://www.police.gov.hk/info/appeals_public/nsc/en.xml); [HK01](https://www.hk01.com/突發/915321/國安處懸紅100萬-被通緝8人身處澳美英-消息-盼收集港同黨資料)). Nobody has been brought before a Hong Kong court, so the condition cannot have been met. That is an inference from the terms. No source states outright that nothing has been paid.

## Lawyers, legislators, organisers and reporters: who the targets are

Grouped by the work they did, the people named form a cross-section of Hong Kong public life before 2020. There are former elected representatives (Law, Dennis Kwok, Ted Hui, Carmen Lau), lawyers (Yam, Dennis Kwok, Hui), a labour leader (Mung), a pollster and social-policy scholar (Chung Kim-wah), journalists and media figures (Ho, Tay, Fok, Choi, Yuen, Gong), student organisers (Siu, Frances Hui, Tony Chung, Cheung), international lobbyists (Anna Kwok, Finn Lau), and the people who organised or won seats in an unofficial diaspora vote. In almost every case the Hong Kong government's allegation concerns speech, testimony, lobbying for sanctions or that vote. When they answered publicly, most said three things: the bounty is intimidation, they will continue, and their host governments should act.

### Round 1, 3 July 2023

**Nathan Law 羅冠聰** led students in the 2014 Umbrella Movement and co-founded and chaired Demosistō. In 2016, at 23, he became the youngest person ever elected to the Legislative Council, and he was disqualified over his oath in 2017. He left Hong Kong on 2 July 2020, the day after the National Security Law took effect, and announced UK asylum in April 2021 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Law)). In exile he testified to the US Congress, was named to the TIME100 in 2020 ([TIME](https://time.com/collection/100-most-influential-people-2020/5888201/nathan-law/)), and wrote *Freedom: How We Lose It and How We Fight Back* (2021) and *When the Wind Blows* (2024). The Hong Kong government alleges incitement to secession and collusion. After the bounty he said: "There will be people who are incentivized to give out my information, my whereabouts" ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/china-hong-kong-democracy-activists-dissidents-bounty-nathan-law-rcna92512)), and: "I am just a Hong Konger speaking out for Hong Kongers – that's all." ([CNN via KRDO](https://krdo.com/?p=992212)). Singapore refused him entry in September 2025, saying his presence "would not be in Singapore's national interests" ([CNA](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/singapore/hong-kong-activist-nathan-law-denied-entry-national-interests-5374966)). *A further X-post quote, "I ask Hongkongers not to cooperate with any related pursuit or bounty actions", comes only from search excerpts. Check it against the original post.*

**Dennis Kwok 郭榮鏗** is a barrister and a founding member of the Civic Party. He represented the Legal functional constituency in LegCo from 2012 until he was disqualified on 11 November 2020, after which 15 pro-democracy colleagues resigned in protest ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Kwok)). In the US he took up scholarly posts at Georgetown and Harvard Kennedy School from 2021 and returned to legal practice. The New York City Bar and New York State Bar both condemned his warrant ([NYC Bar](https://www.nycbar.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Hong_Kong_Bounties.pdf); [NYSBA](https://nysba.org/?p=143552)). The Hong Kong government alleges collusion. The research found no public response from him to the bounty, in English or Chinese.

**Ted Hui 許智峯** is a solicitor who served as a district councillor from 2012 and as a Democratic Party legislator for Hong Kong Island from 2016 to 2020. He left in December 2020 while on bail over a 2019 protest, and moved to Australia in 2021, where he was admitted as a solicitor in South Australia in 2023 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Hui)). He and his family were granted protection visas in August 2025 ([ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-08-17/ted-hui-granted-asylum-protection-in-australia/105664062)). The Hong Kong government alleges incitement to secession, incitement to subversion and collusion. His response: "I think the bounty itself is hilarious. It's ridiculous", "I will continue to live an ordinary, normal life", and "free countries will not extradite us." ([SBS](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/why-has-hong-kong-put-a-bounty-on-these-activists-in-australia/1sa7o4c7c)).

**Kevin Yam 任建峰**, an Australian citizen, spent more than 17 years in Hong Kong as a financial-regulatory and litigation lawyer and co-founded the Progressive Lawyers Group in 2015 ([Lawfare bio](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/contributors/kyam); [Wikipedia: PLG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Lawyers_Group)). He moved back to Australia in 2022 and is a senior fellow at Georgetown Law's Center for Asian Law. He has testified to the Congressional-Executive Commission on China. The Hong Kong government alleges he met foreign officials to push for sanctions on Hong Kong officials, judges and prosecutors ([SBS](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/why-has-hong-kong-put-a-bounty-on-these-activists-in-australia/1sa7o4c7c)). He said: "It's my duty ... to continue to speak out against the crackdown that is going on right now" ([SBS](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/why-has-hong-kong-put-a-bounty-on-these-activists-in-australia/1sa7o4c7c)). After reward letters reached his neighbours in 2025 he said: "I will continue to live my everyday life." / "I will not voluntarily return to Hong Kong before it is free." / "I will not kill myself." ([ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-03-18/melbourne-residents-receive-letter-offering-money-kevin-yam/105065392)).

**Elmer Yuen 袁弓夷** is a businessman and online current-affairs commentator who lobbied Washington for sanctions in 2019–20 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_Yuen)). In 2022, with Victor Ho, he co-led the election committee that became the "Hong Kong Parliament" ([HKFP explainer](https://hongkongfp.com/2024/12/28/explainer-who-are-the-six-overseas-activists-facing-new-hong-kong-nat-security-arrest-warrants/)). Of the 2025 warrant he said in a Facebook live broadcast: "It helps us with a lot of advertising" ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/world/hong-kong-rewards-exiles-canada-1.7594040)). *Wikipedia's "Japanese" nationality descriptor is unverified. ## The law reaches abroad, but enforcement lands at home

The legal machinery has four layers. Each was added once the previous one could not reach its targets.

**Extraterritorial offences.** Article 37 of the 2020 National Security Law applies the law to Hong Kong permanent residents and bodies acting abroad. Article 38 applies it to "offences under this Law committed against the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region from outside the Region by a person who is not a permanent resident of the Region" ([NSL text, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Law_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China_on_Safeguarding_National_Security_in_the_Hong_Kong_Special_Administrative_Region); verify on e-Legislation, A406N). Together they make almost any overseas critic chargeable. That is how foreign citizens such as Feng Chongyi, Sasha Gong, Joey Siu, Victor Ho and Joe Tay came to be named. Officials call this "protective jurisdiction", which they describe as "well-recognised" in international law ([HKFP, 4 Jul 2023](https://hongkongfp.com/2023/07/04/western-countries-decry-hong-kongs-nat-sec-arrest-warrants-as-leader-john-lee-defends-laws-extraterritorial-reach/)). The UN Human Rights Committee had already, in 2022, faulted "The lack of clarity on the grounds for invoking extraterritorial application of the Law" and called for the NSL's repeal ([CCPR/C/CHN-HKG/CO/4](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4002782/files/CCPR_C_CHN-HKG_CO_4-EN.pdf)).

**Warrants and rewards, with rhetoric of lifelong pursuit.** On 4 July 2023 Chief Executive John Lee said: "The only way to end their destiny of being an abscondee who will be pursued for life is to surrender." ([AP via Philstar](https://www.philstar.com/world/2023/07/05/2278831/hong-kong-leader-says-wanted-activists-should-give-or-live-in-fear); [info.gov.hk transcript](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202307/04/P2023070400316.htm)). A week later he said the public should treat the eight as "street rats" (過街老鼠) to be avoided ([HKFP, *snippet*](https://hongkongfp.com/2023/07/11/treat-hong-kongs-8-wanted-democrats-like-street-rats-who-should-be-avoided-at-all-costs-leader-john-lee-says/)). That phrase belongs to Lee in July 2023. No "rats" quote was found for later rounds. Every release cites NSL Articles 37–38, and the 2025 release adds Article 33 (lighter penalties for surrender) and warns that funding or aiding participants, including online, is illegal ([info.gov.hk](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202507/25/P2025072500736.htm)).

**The SNSO "specified absconder" regime.** The Safeguarding National Security Ordinance passed 89–0 on 19 March 2024 and took effect on 23 March. Under its sections 89–98, the Secretary for Security may, by Gazette notice, specify anyone under a national-security arrest warrant who is believed to be abroad, and then apply chosen measures ([SNSO Part 7, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Safeguarding_National_Security_Ordinance/Part_7)). Three of the measures create crimes for *other people*, each carrying up to **7 years**. Section 90 bans making funds available to the absconder or dealing with their funds. Section 91 bans leasing property to or from them. Section 92 bans joint ventures or partnerships with them. Four further measures act on the absconder by deeming: suspension of professional qualifications (s.93), lapse of business or employment permissions (s.94), removal from directorships (s.95), and cancellation of HKSAR passports (s.96). The draft bill's six-month wait between warrant and specification was removed at committee stage after pro-Beijing lawmakers called it "too lenient" ([HKFP](https://hongkongfp.com/2024/03/14/article-23-hong-kong-seeks-to-tighten-absconder-measures-and-empower-leader-to-draft-new-laws/)). Some NGO and congressional summaries still describe the six-month rule; they appear to reflect the draft. The research found **no appeal or review route** for a specified person. The only exits are revocation by the Secretary or a licence from the Secretary (a lay reading of the text that a lawyer should confirm). The government says the regime is "fully in compliance with the international standard for the protection of human rights" and mirrors what "the United States, the United Kingdom and Canada" do ([info.gov.hk, 14 Jun 2024](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202406/14/P2024061400813.htm)).

**Asset confiscation and group bans.** On 17 February 2025 the Court of First Instance granted a confiscation order against Ted Hui under the NSL Article 43 Implementation Rules. Under those rules the court need only be satisfied that "the absconded defendant could have been convicted". The government said he had passed nearly HK$2.5 million to his mother and wife as gifts. The confiscated sum is reported as HK$800,000, and Hui called the order "absurd" ([info.gov.hk](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202502/18/P2025021800005.htm); [SCMP](https://amp.scmp.com/news/hong-kong/law-and-crime/article/3299071/hong-kong-government-slams-wanted-activist-criticising-hk800000-confiscation)). On 2 December 2025 the Secretary for Security used SNSO s.60 for the first time against overseas groups, banning the Hong Kong Parliament and the Hong Kong Democratic Independence Union. Membership or support now carries up to 14 years ([info.gov.hk](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202512/02/P2025120200269.htm)). Wikipedia gives 24 November 2025, possibly the notice date.

**No route to physical custody.** Canada, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, Germany, France and the US all suspended extradition with Hong Kong in July–August 2020 ([Atlantic Council](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/in-depth-research-reports/issue-brief/countering-chinas-crackdown-on-hong-kong/); [ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2020-07-29/china-suspends-hong-kong-extradition-treaty-canada-australia-uk/12501248)). In July 2025 the UK removed Hong Kong from the Extradition Act 2003 designations ([SI 2025/1214](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/1214/note/made/data.xht?view=snippet&wrap=true)). Its accompanying remark about "case-by-case" consideration alarmed activists until Security Minister Dan Jarvis said the UK "will never allow a situation where Hong Kongers or any other nationality is extradited for politically motivated purposes" ([UK six-monthly report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/six-monthly-report-on-hong-kong-july-to-december-2025/six-monthly-report-on-hong-kong-1-july-to-31-december-2025)). Interpol said in July 2023 that it had received no Red Notice requests for the eight and that its constitution bars political cases ([HKFP, *snippet*](https://hongkongfp.com/2023/07/05/hong-kong-security-chief-slams-wall-st-journal-criticism-of-intl-arrest-warrants-as-interpol-say-no-red-notice-received/)). Hong Kong officials have conceded they cannot arrest the exiles unless they return ([NBC](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/china-hong-kong-democracy-activists-dissidents-bounty-nathan-law-rcna92512)). This is the core logic of the campaign. Jurisdiction is unlimited and enforcement is impossible, so the measures that bite are the ones aimed at property, documents and people inside Hong Kong.

## Families carry the cost the exiles cannot be made to pay

Human Rights Watch says authorities "have sought to intimidate dozens of their family members, primarily by interrogating them", which it calls "collective punishment, prohibited by international human rights law" ([HRW, May 2025](https://www.hrw.org/news/2025/05/04/hong-kong-targeting-exiled-activists-families-escalates)). More than 20 relatives and friends of the first eight were questioned by mid-September 2023 ([HKFP, *summary*](https://hongkongfp.com/2023/09/12/self-exiled-hong-kong-democrat-ted-huis-in-laws-questioned-by-national-security-police-reports/)). **The first conviction of a relative.** Kwok Yin-sang, Anna Kwok's father, was arrested on 30 April 2025 and charged under SNSO s.90 with attempting to deal with an absconder's funds. The case concerned an AIA insurance policy he bought when his daughter was two, which prosecutors said he tried to cancel for about **HK$88,609** ([Reuters via WDEZ](https://wdez.com/?p=914130); [AP via NBC](https://www.nbcnews.com/world/asia/father-us-based-hong-kong-activist-convicted-national-security-law-rcna258491)). He pleaded not guilty and did not testify. On 11 February 2026 the court convicted him, holding that he "must have known" his daughter was an absconder and relying in part on what he said on arrest: "I paid for the policy. She is no longer in Hong Kong, so I canceled it." ([The Standard](https://www.thestandard.com.hk/news/article/324227/Father-of-wanted-fugitive-Anna-Kwok-found-guilty-of-handling-absconders-assets)). On 26 February he was sentenced to **eight months**. The magistrate said, "There is no such thing as … collective punishment" ([Reuters via WDEZ](https://wdez.com/?p=914130); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/02/26/nx-s1-5727437/father-activist-sentenced-hong-kong)). Anna Kwok says the policy was never hers. Her response was: "To sentence my father under the pretext that his actions lowered the 'likelihood' of my return… In truth, this is guilt by blood, this is hostage taking. This is transnational repression." Before the sentence she told Reuters: "Asian dads are not very known for showing affection with hugs and I realized this year, I actually … don't think I've ever hugged my dad since I became an adult" ([Reuters via WDEZ](https://wdez.com/?p=914130)). HRW's Elaine Pearson said: "Prosecuting a democracy advocate's parent is an unlawful form of collective punishment." ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/news/2026/02/11/hong-kong-conviction-of-activists-father-a-grim-milestone)). Amnesty called it a "disturbing escalation" ([Amnesty](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/02/hong-kong-conviction-of-wanted-activists-father-a-disturbing-escalation/)). Sources give his age as 68, 69 or 72, and the judge's title as Acting Principal Magistrate (HKFP) or Deputy Judge (The Standard); prefer the HKFP wording. Earlier funding arrests targeted former Demosistō members in July 2023 and four people in December 2023 over subscription payments to Law and Hui ([PBS/AP](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/hong-kong-police-arrest-4-men-accused-of-supporting-people-overseas-endangering-national-security); [The Standard](https://www.thestandard.com.hk/news/article/211437/Hong-Kong-national-security-police-place-HK1m-bounty-on-another-five-overseas-activists)).

**Letters, deepfakes and surveillance abroad.** In February–March 2025, letters copying the police notices reached neighbours of Carmen Lau and Tony Chung in the UK and residents near places linked to Kevin Yam in Australia. The UK posters carried Lau's photo, address and physical description, and invited people to take her "to the Chinese embassy" ([NBC](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/uk-bounty-hong-kong-democracy-activist-china-rcna194068); [RFA](https://www.rfa.org/english/china/2025/03/05/china-uk-hong-kong-wanted-activists-letters/); [ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-03-18/melbourne-residents-receive-letter-offering-money-kevin-yam/105065392)). The Hong Kong government said it "would not issue anonymous letters", and no sender has been officially identified. Later in 2025 the campaign turned sexual. A sexualised poster of Ted Hui's wife was emailed to his former workplace, and around December letters posted from Macau sent Lau's former neighbours digitally altered explicit images of her. Thames Valley Police are investigating and have made no arrests ([Reuters via MarketScreener](https://uk.marketscreener.com/news/exiled-hong-kong-democracy-activists-say-they-re-targeted-by-fake-sexualised-images-ce7d50dbdf8ff32c)). In Canada, Liberal MP Paul Chiang suggested in January 2025 that people could take Joe Tay to the Chinese consulate to claim the reward. He apologised and withdrew as a candidate in March after the RCMP said it was looking into the remark ([RCI](https://ici.radio-canada.ca/rci/en/news/2152380/liberal-candidate-paul-chiang-withdraws-from-race-after-suggesting-people-claim-chinas-bounty-on-conservative)). The most consequential case was in the UK. On 7 May 2026 an Old Bailey jury convicted HKETO London office manager Chung Biu (Bill) Yuen and former Border Force officer Chi Leung (Peter) Wai of assisting a foreign intelligence service. The CPS said they researched UK-based dissidents, "called 'cockroaches'" by Hong Kong-linked individuals ([CPS](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cps/news/updated-sentence-cps-secures-convictions-hong-kong-unlawful-surveillance-case); [Counter Terrorism Policing](https://www.counterterrorism.police.uk/news/two-men-jailed-for-hong-kong-spying-offences/)). Verify before naming them.*

**The wider community.** A Hong Kong Watch survey of more than 1,000 Hongkongers in the UK found **66%** feel at risk of transnational repression and **86%** believe joining public events in the UK puts family in Hong Kong at risk ([Hong Kong Watch, May 2026](https://www.hongkongwatch.org/all-posts/2026/5/11/new-hong-kong-watch-survey-research-on-tnr-calls-for-enhanced-foreign-interference-measures)). The Hong Kong Democracy Council told Parliament that an "atmosphere of fear has fueled self-censorship, mistrust, hesitation and a pervasive sense of insecurity within the community" ([HKDC evidence TRUK0070](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/137989/pdf/)). The leverage built for 34 people reaches the roughly 220,000 Hongkongers who have moved to the UK on the BN(O) route, and others besides.

**Measures beyond words are few.** On 31 March 2025 the US sanctioned six officials under Executive Order 13936: OSNS director Dong Jingwei, Secretary for Justice Paul Lam, Police Commissioner Raymond Siu, Committee for Safeguarding National Security secretary-general Sonny Au, and national-security assistant commissioners Dick Wong and Margaret Chiu. The UK has sanctioned no one; Iain Duncan Smith told the Commons in March 2026 that "the UK, which used to run Hong Kong, has sanctioned absolutely nobody" ([Hansard](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2026-03-03/debates/C629FE3B-7C63-4C81-B7E2-4C5A4BA98BEF)). The UK's response has instead been protective and legal: summoning the Chinese ambassador (May 2024, and around 8–9 May 2026 after the HKETO convictions), a transnational-repression review, training for all 45 police forces, asylum grants, and the spy prosecution. It has not placed China on the enhanced tier of the Foreign Influence Registration Scheme, and it declined in June 2026 to close the London HKETO ([Hansard Lords](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Lords/2026-06-22/debates/D4163E73-71A7-4E53-AFAB-7A9FD988CB6D)). ## Conclusion

The record shows a campaign that defines its targets by their public work, but can act only on what remains in Hong Kong: passports, pensions, insurance policies, professional licences and, above all, parents. That inversion is the most important fact for a public page. The 34 named people are, almost without exception, safe from arrest and continuing their work. Many have answered in their own voices with defiance or irony. The measurable harm falls on a father in his late sixties or older (reports differ on his age), on siblings and cousins summoned to police stations, and on a diaspora where most people weigh family safety before attending a public event. Presenting the people as lawyers, unionists, scholars, reporters and organisers is not just a matter of framing. It matches the government's own allegations, which describe lobbying, testimony, commentary and a vote.

The second takeaway concerns the response. Condemnation is now near-universal among democracies, but it is triggered by nationality, which is why Canada and the EU spoke only when their own people were named. Coercive cost to the officials running the programme has come only once, from Washington in March 2025. The UK's most effective act was a criminal prosecution of people who spied for Hong Kong on British soil, not a measure aimed at Hong Kong. The Interpol assembly in Hong Kong and the December renewals will show whether that asymmetry holds.
