**Cantonese** (粵語 / 廣東話) is the everyday tongue of the overwhelming majority of Hongkongers — the language of its streets and wet markets, its cinema and Canto-pop, its courtrooms, its classrooms and its protests. By the **2021 census, over 88%** of residents speak Cantonese as their usual language and **close to 94%** can speak it ([HKFP](https://hongkongfp.com/2026/09/22/prestigious-hong-kong-school-withdraws-unapproved-cantonese-ban-following-controversy/)). And yet, again and again, the institutions that govern the city have treated it as negotiable — a "dialect," a thing to be set aside as Mandarin rises. This collection tracks that slow squeeze, and the people who refuse to let the mother tongue go quiet.

## A language, not a "dialect"

Whether Cantonese is a "language" or a mere "dialect" of Chinese is, for Hongkongers, a question of identity as much as linguistics — and the answer carries political weight. If Cantonese is "just" a dialect, its exclusion from education and officialdom might be waved through; if it is a language in its own right, favouring Putonghua at its expense "smacks of politics."

By the linguists' test — mutual comprehensibility — Cantonese is **not** a dialect of Mandarin: the two are about as different as two Romance languages, and Cantonese is spoken by roughly as many people as Italian. As the oft-quoted line attributed to linguist Max Weinreich has it, "a language is a dialect with an army and a navy." Cantonese also carries a longer literary lineage than Putonghua — the SCMP columnist **Alex Lo** notes it may trace to Tang-dynasty speech, citing the scholar Nan Huai-chin that "Hakka and Cantonese were the Tang dynasty's national languages… Putonghua is a [recent] national language" ([China Digital Times](https://chinadigitaltimes.net/2014/02/cantonese-dialect-language/)).

## When institutions treat the mother tongue as optional

### 2014 — "a Chinese dialect that is not an official language"

In **January 2014**, the **Education Bureau** posted an article on its *Language Learning Support* page that, while promoting bilingualism and trilingualism as the city "develops alongside the rapidly growing China," stated: "Although the Basic Law stipulates that Chinese and English are the two official languages in Hong Kong, nearly 97 per cent of the local population learn Cantonese (**a Chinese dialect that is not an official language**) as their commonly used daily language."

The line drew a storm of criticism. The Basic Law names "Chinese" and English as official languages but defines no spoken standard; as **Eric Cheung Tat-ming**, principal law lecturer at the University of Hong Kong, pointed out, "there have never been attempts to define what official languages mean in the oral context," and in Hong Kong's courts and official settings "the choice of the spoken language has been one based on commonality, so Cantonese is preferred." Within days the Education Bureau **deleted the article** ([China Digital Times](https://chinadigitaltimes.net/2014/02/cantonese-dialect-language/) · [SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/news/hong-kong/article/1419237/education-bureau-rapped-over-cantonese-not-official-language-gaffe)).

### 2026 — a school brands Cantonese "strictly forbidden"

On **22 September 2026**, the prestigious girls' school **Good Hope School** (德望學校) issued a full withdrawal of a graduation-video production guideline that had declared **Cantonese "STRICTLY FORBIDDEN"** for speech, dialogue and voiceovers — students "must use English or Mandarin." Screenshots of the guideline had gone viral on Threads over the weekend.

Reversing course, the school said it bore "**full responsibility**," that language "carries our memories, emotions, affections and is the living soul of our heritage," and that it had "never been our ethos to constrain the mother tongue of any child entrusted to our care." Principal **Gary Harfitt** apologised, calling the slide an unrevised draft that did not reflect school policy — though the statement did not explain why the guideline had been issued. The **Education Bureau** said it had urged the school to review its event arrangements but **declined to comment on the school's language policy** ([HKFP](https://hongkongfp.com/2026/09/22/prestigious-hong-kong-school-withdraws-unapproved-cantonese-ban-following-controversy/)).

## Keeping Cantonese alive

The pressure on Cantonese is rarely a ban; more often it is a quiet reweighting — Mandarin framed as the language of the future, Cantonese as sentiment. But the mother tongue has proved stubborn. It remains the voice of Hong Kong's culture and of its civic life: the protest anthem [[Glory to Hong Kong|glory-to-hong-kong]] was written and sung in Cantonese, and Cantonese-language journalism and film (see [[Hong Kong protest films|films]]) keep the idiom in the public record. Among the diaspora, Cantonese classes, reading groups and recordings have become a form of preservation in themselves.
