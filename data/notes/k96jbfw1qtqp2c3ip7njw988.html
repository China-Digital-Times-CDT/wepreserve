⚖️ Joshua Wong|黃之鋒

Related: Faces of the Crackdown | The Hong Kong 47 | Hong Kong Alliance | Chow Hang-tung | ⚖️ Jimmy Lai

Joshua Wong Chi-fung in 2017. Photo: Voice of America, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Joshua Wong Chi-fung (黃之鋒, born 13 October 1996) is the best-known figure of Hong Kong's youth protest movement — the schoolboy activist who became the global face of the 2014 Umbrella Movement and, a decade later, one of the most heavily prosecuted people under the National Security Law. He has been in custody since 2020, is serving a sentence in the Hong Kong 47 subversion case, and on 5 November 2026 was given a further 10 years after pleading guilty to a second national-security charge — foreign collusion — which the court found "of a grave nature."

The student who took on Beijing

Wong first came to prominence at 15. In 2011 he co-founded the student group Scholarism (學民思潮); in 2012 he led the campaign against a compulsory "moral and national education" curriculum critics saw as pro-Beijing indoctrination, drawing a rally of some 100,000 people and forcing the government to shelve the plan.

Two years later he was a leading face of the 2014 Umbrella Movement, the 79-day occupation demanding genuine universal suffrage. His arrest during the storming of Civic Square helped trigger the sit-ins. The image of a bespectacled teenager facing down Beijing made him internationally famous — named to Time's "25 Most Influential Teens," later the subject of the Netflix documentary Joshua: Teenager vs. Superpower (2017), and in 2018 nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize alongside fellow Umbrella leaders.

Demosistō and disqualification

In 2016 Wong co-founded the pro-democracy party Demosistō (香港眾志) with Nathan Law and Agnes Chow, campaigning for Hong Kong's right to self-determination. He was repeatedly barred from running for or holding office. On 30 June 2020, hours before the National Security Law took effect, Wong, Law and Chow quit the party and Demosistō disbanded — its parting message: "We will meet again."

A decade in and out of prison

Even before the NSL, Wong was jailed again and again over the protests he led — for the 2014 storming of Civic Square, for contempt over the Mong Kok occupation, for organising the June 2019 siege of police headquarters during the anti-extradition-bill movement, and for attending banned June 4th vigils. He has been continuously imprisoned since December 2020.

The Hong Kong 47

Wong was among the Hong Kong 47 — the democrats prosecuted for organising the 2020 opposition primary election. He pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit subversion and, in November 2024, was sentenced to 4 years 8 months. He had been due for release in 2027.

A second case — another ten years

On 6 June 2025, while already in prison, Wong was hit with a fresh charge: conspiracy to collude with foreign forces. Prosecutors say that in 2020 he lobbied foreign governments — chiefly the United States — to impose sanctions or a blockade on Hong Kong and China, and kept up that campaign after the NSL made it a crime.

On 2 September 2026 Wong pleaded guilty to the charge at the High Court. The offence carries three to ten years, rising to up to life imprisonment if the court finds it "of a grave nature" — meaning the 29-year-old, rather than walking free in 2027, could face many more years behind bars (HKFP · Al Jazeera · RFA).

On 5 November 2026, at the High Court before NSL-designated judge William Tam Yiu-ho (譚耀豪), Wong — who had turned 30 in October and been remanded over 2,100 days — was sentenced to 10 years' imprisonment. In mitigation his senior counsel Chan Ching-lung (陳政龍) had argued the case was less grave than Jimmy Lai's and belonged in the ordinary 3-to-10-year band, with a one-third discount for the guilty plea and a further reduction for the prosecution's delay (the probe opened only in late 2023). The judge rejected that framing: he ruled the offence "of a grave nature" (罪行重大) — the finding that opens the 10-years-to-life band — holding that Wong and Nathan Law had continued their sanctions campaign after the NSL took effect, gravely endangering national security. Tam took a starting point of 13 years and, crediting the early plea, came down to the 10-year statutory minimum that a grave offence cannot fall below. Only 2 months and 2 days of the term runs concurrently with Wong's Hong Kong 47 sentence; the remainder is consecutive. Having been due for release in January 2027, he is now expected to remain imprisoned until November 2036, when he will be 40 (HKFP · CNN · The Witness · HK01).

In the public gallery, consular officials from Sweden, Italy, the Netherlands and the EU looked on; veteran activist Lei Yuk-lin (雷玉蓮), who had queued for a seat since the Tuesday, carried a gold heart-shaped candle for Wong, and the 102-year-old "Uncle Wong" (大黃伯) was again among those lining up before dawn (The Witness).

The reaction

The sentence drew a coordinated official response. Chief Executive John Lee (李家超) called Wong, the exiled Nathan Law and their affiliates "among the primary culprits responsible for undermining Hong Kong's prosperity and stability," accusing them of begging foreign powers to sanction the city; a government spokesperson said the ruling shattered the illusion that security offenders could escape liability under the cloak of "international advocacy," and that the authorities would confiscate property related to Wong's offence (Dim Sum Daily · The Standard). Security Secretary Chris Tang (鄧炳強) said the term reflected the offence's gravity and that the government would study the judgment with the Department of Justice before deciding whether to appeal; he reserved his "strongest condemnation" for the "external forces" criticising the verdict — "the very forces," he said, "Wong and Law tried to collude with." Asked whether Wong could join in-custody rehabilitation, Tang said "of course," pointing to a prisoners' "Walking With You" (陪你前行) programme he hoped would help Wong grasp "how he was incited and sold out by the US and the West" (Now News). National-security police chief Steve Li Kwai-wah (李桂華) said the investigation — opened on 2023 intelligence and charged two years later — had not been delayed, urged Wong to "properly understand his own country," and called on Nathan Law, now in the UK, to return and surrender.

The official framing did not go unchallenged. In a statement sent from prison, Wong said a national-security officer had told him they were "enemies"; asked about it, Li replied he personally would not regard Wong as an enemy — "He is an offender." Amnesty International's deputy regional director Sarah Brooks said the sentence "cruelly — and perhaps deliberately — comes mere months before he would have been eligible for release," called the security law "a tool to punish dissent rather than to genuinely protect national security," and renewed Amnesty's call to overturn Wong's convictions; Human Rights Watch's Asia director Elaine Pearson likewise called the sentence "cruel and unjust" (HKFP).

Abroad, the sentence drew a coordinated rebuke. On 6 November 2026 the foreign ministries of ten governments — Australia, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the United States — issued a joint statement calling for an end to the unjustified prosecution of people under the national security law and urging Beijing and Hong Kong to honour the rights guaranteed by the 1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration (Australia DFAT · Italy MFA). Several governments and bodies also spoke on their own: the European Union urged the authorities to respect the ICCPR and the Joint Declaration; the UK Foreign Office demanded "an immediate end to all unjust prosecutions" under the law; the US Consulate said Wong had been "unjustly imprisoned since December 2020"; and Taiwan — its Presidential Office (spokesperson Karen Kuo) and Mainland Affairs Council — said "seeking democracy should not be a crime" and called for his release (HKFP · RFA). The Hong Kong government condemned the joint statement as interference in China's internal affairs and an attempt to pressure its courts, insisting the case had been handled strictly according to law, while Beijing's foreign ministry (spokesperson Guo Jiakun 郭嘉昆) said the central authorities "firmly support" the city in safeguarding national security (Dim Sum Daily · Nikkei).

The international press read the sentence as a verdict on the West as much as on Wong. Germany's Süddeutsche Zeitung called him "the symbol of an entire generation" — the young who took to the streets with umbrellas and would not give up their freedom — and argued that the world's weak response had effectively handed Beijing a free pass, teaching its leaders that breaking international law carries almost no cost. Die Welt accused the West of abandoning its freedom fighters, writing that a figure like Wong understood the worth of Western liberty better than much of its own complacent establishment (via RFI · archived).

Even the film is under review (2026)

Wong's story reached a global audience through Joshua: Teenager vs. Superpower (2017), the 78-minute Netflix documentary by American director Joe Piscatella that follows his Scholarism years and features Nathan Law and Agnes Chow. Nearly a decade on, the film itself became a target. On 15 September 2026, Hong Kong's Security Bureau said it was "taking follow-up action" over the documentary — still streamable in the city, rated 13+ — stressing that "all films broadcast in the city must meet Hong Kong law," including the National Security Law and the 2024 Safeguarding National Security Ordinance. Whether any act amounts to a crime, it said, "depends on the facts and circumstances of each case" and "cannot be judged in the abstract"; if violations were found, "law-enforcement agencies will act according to the law" (Dim Sum Daily).

Back in 2020, as the NSL took effect, Netflix had signalled it would not "proactively" pull the film and would act only on a formal government takedown request (HKFP). The review extends the city's censorship drive — already felt by its bookshops and the Tiananmen vigil — to an international streaming platform, and raised the prospect that merely hosting a film about a jailed activist could be cast as a security offence. The film sits within the archive's wider record of Hong Kong protest films.

Recognition

Wong's activism made him one of the movement's most decorated figures abroad — Time's most-influential-teens list (2014), Fortune's "World's Greatest Leaders" (2015), a 2018 Nobel Peace Prize nomination, and the co-author of Unfree Speech: The Threat to Global Democracy and Why We Must Act, Now (2020), with a foreword by last colonial governor Chris Patten and an introduction by artist Ai Weiwei. To Beijing he became a symbol of "foreign-backed" subversion; to his supporters, of a generation jailed for asking to choose its own government.
