Harvard's Schlesinger Library holds a **~73-minute audio recording of a lecture, "Chinese Translation: A Vehicle of Cultural Influence,"** attributed to **Eileen Chang** (張愛玲, 1920–1995) — the Shanghai-born novelist of *Love in a Fallen City* (傾城之戀) and *Lust, Caution* (色，戒), who fled the People's Republic for **Hong Kong in 1952** and later settled in the United States. She gave the talk as a **Radcliffe Institute fellow (1967–69)**, under her married name **Reyher**.

**Listen at Harvard (streaming):** [Bunting Institute Seminars — "Reyher" track](https://mps.lib.harvard.edu/sds/audio/503996508) · persistent link [URN-3:RAD.SCHL:106426192](https://nrs.lib.harvard.edu/URN-3:RAD.SCHL:106426192) · finding aid [sch01475](https://hollisarchives.lib.harvard.edu/catalog/sch01475).

## The recording

- **Speaker:** attributed to Eileen Chang (張愛玲) — see the caveat below.
- **Title:** "Chinese Translation: A Vehicle of Cultural Influence" (the recording's chair; an academic account gives the variant "…Cultural Exchange").
- **Date:** **1 April 1969**, at the Radcliffe Institute for Independent Study, Cambridge, Massachusetts.
- **Length:** 1:12:46 — a ~50-minute lecture followed by ~20 minutes of audience questions.
- **Subject:** the history of Chinese literary translation — **Lin Shu** (林紓) and his rendering of Rider Haggard, **Yan Fu** (嚴復), **Lu Xun** (魯迅), and how translation was bent to state policy on the mainland after 1949.
- **Held by:** Schlesinger Library, Radcliffe Institute, Harvard University. Identifier **RA.T-106** ("Bunting Institute Seminars. Reyher; Bas-Cohain, n.d.").

### On the attribution

Harvard's catalogue does **not** name Eileen Chang; it credits the track only to **"Reyher"** — the surname of Chang's American husband, the playwright Ferdinand Reyher (d. 1967). The identification rests on: (1) the companion track's host announcing the following week's speaker as **"Eileen Chang Reyher"** with this exact title; (2) Chang's documented **Radcliffe Institute fellowship (1967–69)** under that married name; and (3) convergent outside sources describing a **1 April 1969 Radcliffe lecture of this title and 1h12m length** as hers — a [Lingnan University](https://commons.ln.edu.hk/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1288&context=jmlc) academic article, a [USC Libraries exhibit](https://scalar.usc.edu/works/eileen-chang-exhibit/media/untitled-speech-radcliffe-institute-1969), and October 2026 Chinese-language press ([United Daily](https://uniteddaily.my/en/6c8f64c3-7ffa-4b18-aa6b-67463a51bc8b/%E5%BC%A0%E7%88%B1%E7%8E%B2%E7%BD%95%E8%A7%81%E6%BC%94%E8%AE%B2%E5%8E%9F%E5%A3%B0%E5%BD%95%E9%9F%B3%E5%9C%A8%E4%B8%AD%E6%96%87%E4%BA%92%E8%81%94%E7%BD%91%E7%83%AD%E4%BC%A0)) — all matching this track. The identification is now widely made and well-supported, though **Harvard's own catalogue still files it only under "Reyher."**

## Why it belongs in this archive

Eileen Chang's life traces the same escape route this archive keeps returning to. She wrote **Shanghai and Hong Kong** as twinned cities, left the mainland when the new political order made her kind of writing impossible, and spent her last decades abroad — her Shanghai work circulating for years only in **banned or pirated editions**. Her subject in this very lecture — how a state rewrites what may be translated and read — is the same machinery that later reached the newsrooms, bookshops and vigils documented across this site (see [[📕 Book Censorship under the NSL|book-censorship]] and [[📰 Press Freedom under Siege|press-freedom]]).

Her two cities have since met again in the headlines: **Shanghai's 2022 Covid lockdown** and the **"white paper" / A4 protests** (白紙運動) that it triggered, and Hong Kong's own closing space. A writer who fled one silencing, recorded here explaining how translation outlives it, is worth preserving alongside them — which is the point of the archive's **[[Extra|extra]]** collection.

## In her words | 她的話

Short passages from Chang's own writing, with Chinese translation. More — from the **1969 Radcliffe lecture** — will be added here once verified against the published PMLA text (not the machine transcript of the audio).

**On Communist rule and nationalism** — from her **1965** autobiographical statement for *World Authors 1950–1970*, on why, after "the final disintegration of ingrown latter-day Confucianism," Communist rule could feel familiar to many Chinese:

> "To many others Communist rule is also more palatable for being a reversion to the old order, only replacing the family with the larger blood kin, the state, **incorporating nationalism, the undisputed religion of our time**."
>
> 「對另外許多人來說，共產黨的統治也更容易接受，因為它回到了舊秩序：只是用更大的血緣共同體——國家——取代家庭，**並吸納了民族主義，這個我們時代無可爭議的宗教**。」
>
> — *World Authors 1950–1970*, ed. John Wakeman (H. W. Wilson, 1975; written 1965); Chinese after 高全之, 《張愛玲學》 (麥田, 2003).

## A journalist's take: Chai Jing (2026)

The independent journalist **Chai Jing** (柴靜) — the former CCTV reporter whose 2015 pollution documentary *Under the Dome* (穹頂之下) drew 300 million views before censors pulled it, and who now runs one of the most-watched overseas Chinese-language current-affairs channels on YouTube — devoted an October 2026 episode to Chang and this newly surfaced recording: **「周恩來為什麼沒留住張愛玲？｜張愛玲錄音公開」** ("Why didn't Zhou Enlai keep Eileen Chang? | Eileen Chang's recording made public"). It frames Chang's 1952 departure from the mainland against the new state's wish to retain its writers, and has **drawn online debate**. The recording itself has **spread widely across the Chinese-language internet** since mid-October 2026 — full copies and clips reposted by netizens on **Weibo** and **Xiaohongshu** — though it remains a copyrighted Schlesinger holding, not an openly licensed one ([United Daily, 18 Oct 2026](https://uniteddaily.my/en/6c8f64c3-7ffa-4b18-aa6b-67463a51bc8b/%E5%BC%A0%E7%88%B1%E7%8E%B2%E7%BD%95%E8%A7%81%E6%BC%94%E8%AE%B2%E5%8E%9F%E5%A3%B0%E5%BD%95%E9%9F%B3%E5%9C%A8%E4%B8%AD%E6%96%87%E4%BA%92%E8%81%94%E7%BD%91%E7%83%AD%E4%BC%A0)).

## Rights — why this page only links

Reproducing the **audio** or a **full transcript** needs permission we do not yet have:

- The **Schlesinger Library states it does not hold copyright in most of its audiovisual material** and places responsibility on the user to identify and clear rights with the actual holder, and to contact a librarian before publishing ([permissions & copyright](https://www.radcliffe.harvard.edu/schlesinger-library/research-services/permissions-and-copyright)).
- The lecture is **Eileen Chang's own spoken work**, so copyright in her words rests with her **estate** (Chang died in 1995; the estate is administered by Roland Soong / 宋以朗). A full transcript or the audio would need the **estate's permission**, independent of Harvard.
- A rough machine transcript exists as a working draft but has errors on names and in the Q&A and has **not** been corrected against the audio.

**So, pending clearance, this page:** links to Harvard's streaming page (always safe), describes the recording in our own words, and credits the Schlesinger Library as the source. To go further — host the audio, publish a corrected transcript, or quote at length — we would first ask the **Chang estate** and the **Schlesinger Library**.
